Substance Use Disorder (SUD) Toolkit

Prerequisites

Diagnosis: Establish probable diagnosis of alcohol withdrawal using DSM-5 or ICD-11 . It is crucial to rule out other medical conditions that can mimic withdrawal, such as sepsis, metabolic disturbances, or intracranial events.

BAL (Blood Alcohol Level): Not a contraindication to starting treatment. It is preferable to begin dosing when the BAL is low or falling (e.g., < 0.1 g/dL), which helps differentiate intoxication from true withdrawal - but a rising score with a falling BAL is itself the signal to treat, and withdrawal may begin before the BAL reaches zero. There is no fixed number of hours since the last drink that must elapse first. NSWCG §5.1, §5.3

Liver Function: Assess liver function (LFTs, INR, Albumin) to guide benzodiazepine choice. Severe liver impairment may necessitate using a drug with no active metabolites, like Oxazepam.

CIWA-Ar Scale: Commence CIWA-Ar monitoring every 2-4 hours. This objective scale guides symptom-triggered therapy, allowing for tailored dosing and preventing both under- and over-sedation. Scoring is not withheld pending a time interval; see "Interpreting CIWA-Ar early" on the Assessment & Banding tab for how to read a score taken while the patient is still intoxicated.

Post-withdrawal plan: Ensure there is a follow-up plan in place for ongoing support and monitoring after the withdrawal process. To withdraw without a plan is to risk (1) relapse and broader treatment futility (2) reducing alcohol tolerance and temporarily worsening risks of intoxication.

Thiamine

WKS prophylaxis for an otherwise healthy person with good dietary intake (generally not appropriate for those receiving significant withdrawal treatment): Oral thiamine 100mg TDS (or 300mg daily) for 3 days then 100mg daily for 11 days thereafter

WKS prophylaxis for those with chronic high level alcohol use and/or poor nutrition: All patients undergoing inpatient alcohol withdrawal should receive parenteral thiamine. Commence thiamine 300mg daily IV (preferred) or IM for 3 days, then 300mg oral daily for 2-3 weeks. Crucially, always administer thiamine BEFORE any glucose-containing fluids.

Route - prefer IV over IM. Alcohol is associated with thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy that may render intramuscular injection unsafe. Check platelets and coagulation before giving thiamine IM, and prefer the IV route (by the time you do this, a cannula may be in situ anyway!) NSWCG §5.4.7

Suspected Wernicke-Korsakov Syndrome (WKS): Have a high index of suspicion for WKS in any patient with confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, memory disturbance, or malnutrition. The classic triad is rare. Treatment requires high-dose thiamine: 500mg IV TDS for a minimum of 5 days (i.e. 7.5g) or otherwise until symptoms resolve or improvement plateaus, followed by oral supplementation.

Caveat: Patients must be magnesium replete for adequate thiamine absorption and activation. NSWCG §5.4.7

1. Benzodiazepine Choice

Diazepam: The standard choice due to its long half-life, which provides a smooth, self-tapering effect and reduces the risk of breakthrough symptoms and seizures.

Oxazepam: Preferred in specific situations as it has no active metabolites and is less likely to accumulate.

Use Oxazepam in cases of:
• Significant liver impairment (e.g., cirrhosis, high bilirubin, coagulopathy).
• Respiratory insufficiency or risk of respiratory depression (e.g., COPD).
• Elderly or frail patients who are more sensitive to sedative effects.
• Cerebral trauma / CVA where over-sedation is a risk.

Conversion: Diazepam 10mg is approximately equivalent to Oxazepam 30mg. NSWCG Table 11.2

Current Selection: Diazepam

2. Assessment & Banding

Interpreting CIWA-Ar early

Check BAC before commencing. CIWA-Ar is unreliable while the patient is intoxicated - intoxication and withdrawal features overlap. A rising score with a falling BAL is the signal to treat. Withdrawal usually begins 6-24 hours after the last drink but may begin before the BAL reaches zero, and in severely dependent drinkers a reduction in intake alone can precipitate it. NSWCG §5.1, §5.3

Do not delay treatment on time-since-last-drink alone where the patient is in objective withdrawal, or has a history of withdrawal seizures or alcohol withdrawal delirium. NSWCG §5.6.1

Ensure CIWA-Ar is done q2hrly at least initially for all patients commenced on a regimen. Regularly review the patient and adjust as per clinical judgement.

Choosing a band

The standard-drink split used on the Regimens tab is the default entry point. LOCAL - rationale: the ≤14 / ≥15 standard drinks per day split is not from NSWCG, which states severity is difficult to predict (§5.1.1) and gives risk factors rather than an intake threshold; the split is retained because it is usable at the bedside and matches local practice.

Move the patient up a band regardless of reported intake if any of the following NSWCG risk factors are present: NSWCG §5.1.1

  • Previous severe withdrawal.
  • Higher BAL on arrival, or presenting already in withdrawal.
  • Coexisting medical conditions.
  • Seizures early in withdrawal.
  • Co-occurring dependence on other CNS depressants.

Escalation triggers

Escalate - move up a schedule and request medical review if any of:

  • Both daily PRN doses have been used and the patient remains symptomatic.
  • Two consecutive CIWA-Ar (or AWS) scores in the band above the current schedule.
  • CIWA-Ar (or AWS) rising on days 3-4 of an established taper.
  • Total diazepam ≥ 80mg in 24 hours → medical officer review to exclude other pathology. NSWCG §5.4.4
  • Any seizure, delirium, hallucinations, or hyperthermia → treat as severe; see Special Cases.

De-escalate / withhold if sedated: do not give regular or PRN doses if the patient is sedated - withhold the dose and review the regular schedule. If multiple doses are withheld, the schedule is too high.

Consider increasing the regimen if PRN is being used frequently. A patient who keeps needing PRN doses and remains symptomatic needs a different schedule, not a longer wait. LOCAL - rationale: NSWCG gives escalation thresholds but no exit criteria from a fixed schedule, and the Mild-Mod band's higher PRN trigger (CIWA 15-20) overlaps the Mod-Sev band entirely, so a patient scoring 17 could otherwise stay on a 10mg qid backbone indefinitely.

⚠️ Delayed or Uncertain Commencement: If the commencement of withdrawal is significantly delayed or the timeline is highly uncertain (e.g., last drink was >24-48 hours ago, or patient history is unreliable), routine initiation of a weaning regimen is cautioned. Seeking specialist clinical advice first and considering a test-dose protocol to assess tolerance is advised.

If the taper is incomplete at discharge

Staged supply - 24 hours of medication at a time. Self-medication is a risk during alcohol withdrawal, particularly with minimal supervision. NSWCG §5.4.4, App 6

Inform the patient of the risks of taking the remainder of the taper unsupervised: overdose, undiagnosed complications, and failure to complete the withdrawal. NSWCG §5.4.4

Limit benzodiazepine use to 5-7 days. NSWCG §5.4.4

Special Cases

⚠️ Pregnancy: Alcohol withdrawal during pregnancy is high risk for both maternal and fetal complications (including miscarriage, premature labour, and fetal distress). Specialist Obstetric and Addiction Medicine consultation is strongly advised.

Elderly / Frail Patients: High risk for over-sedation, falls, and paradoxical reactions. Routine use of long-acting agents (diazepam) is cautioned. Oxazepam is generally preferred, starting at lower doses with frequent clinical review.

Seizures (current or past)

Early treatment with diazepam (loading) is indicated where there is a history of withdrawal seizures. NSWCG §5.6.1

⚠️ Prophylactic anticonvulsants have no benefit in preventing alcohol withdrawal seizures - this includes phenytoin, carbamazepine and sodium valproate. Assess any seizure for other causes rather than adding an anticonvulsant. NSWCG §5.6.1

Alcohol withdrawal delirium (DT)

🚨 DT is a diagnosis of exclusion. Screen for subdural haematoma, head injury, Wernicke's encephalopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoxia, sepsis, metabolic disturbance, and intoxication with or withdrawal from other drugs. Major psychotic disorders can mimic it. NSWCG §5.6.2

Setting: ICU/HDU or 1:1 nursing. NSWCG §5.6.2

Benzodiazepine: diazepam 20mg hourly up to 80mg/24 hours - often insufficient alone in established DT. If the oral route is not possible: IV midazolam infusion monitored in HDU, or IM lorazepam where no HDU is available. Target: light sleep, readily rousable. NSWCG §5.6.2

⚠️ Counterintuitive - consider reducing, not increasing. If delirium persists despite significant benzodiazepine dosing, consider dose reduction rather than escalation: high-dose benzodiazepines can themselves produce delirium. NSWCG §5.4.4, §5.6.2

If still unsettled after loading: droperidol, or olanzapine 2.5-5mg oral/buccal/SL to a maximum of 20mg/24 hours, continued PRN for several days as needed. NSWCG §5.6.2

Thiamine: IV thiamine 300mg. NSWCG §5.6.2

Supportive care: single room; 1:1 nursing as needed; fluid balance and electrolyte replacement; nutrition; monitor for infection. NSWCG §5.6.2

🚨 Self-discharge is not acceptable. Patients with DT are mentally disordered. NSWCG §5.6.2

Hallucinations

First choice is diazepam. Add olanzapine 2.5-5mg if unresponsive. Persistent psychosis warrants follow-up to recovery. NSWCG §5.6.2

Severe chronic airflow limitation

⚠️ Do not use loading regimens. Use benzodiazepines with caution and close monitoring. An IV midazolam infusion in HDU may be the best option; otherwise short-acting temazepam or oxazepam cautiously, with close respiratory monitoring. NSWCG §5.6.3

Adjunctive Medications for Severe Symptoms: In cases of delirium or severe agitation/psychosis poorly responsive to benzodiazepines, other agents may be considered after specialist consultation. Examples include low-dose antipsychotics (e.g., Haloperidol, Olanzapine). These are not first-line and require careful risk/benefit assessment.

Poly-substance Withdrawal: Overlapping symptoms (e.g., sweating, tachycardia, nausea) from co-occurring opioid or benzodiazepine withdrawal can artificially inflate the CIWA-Ar score. Generally, treat the most life-threatening withdrawal first (Alcohol/Benzodiazepines) with long-acting sedatives, which often incidentally smooths out less dangerous withdrawals (like opioids or stimulants). LOCAL - rationale: NSWCG notes co-occurring dependence on other CNS depressants as a severity risk factor but gives no sequencing rule; this ordering reflects local practice of treating the withdrawal that can kill first.

Exceeding 80mg diazepam in 24 hours

80mg is a review threshold, not a ceiling. Further diazepam may be appropriate once other causes have been excluded, so treat this as a two-stage ladder rather than a stop sign.

  1. 80mg / 24 hours - medical officer review required. Assess for other pathology (see alcohol withdrawal delirium above - it is a diagnosis of exclusion). NSWCG §5.4.4
  2. If no other cause is found and withdrawal persists - consider diazepam 10-20mg 2-hourly PRN, to a maximum of 120mg in 24 hours. NSWCG §5.4.4
  3. Persistent agitation or hallucinations, or more than 120mg / 24 hours - specialist advice: D&A CL service, or the Drug and Alcohol State Advisory Service (DASAS) on 1800 023 687 (regional, rural and remote NSW) or (02) 8382 1006 (Sydney metropolitan area), or the on-call addiction medicine specialist or addiction psychiatrist. NSWCG §5.4.4, §2.6

Patient Selection Criteria

The following criteria should be met for a patient to be considered for an outpatient (ambulatory) detox.

Inclusion Criteria

  • Age > 18-years-old.
  • Has undertaken a comprehensive AOD assessment, has alcohol dependence, and is willing to engage in the detox process.
  • Patient has up-to-date FBC, LFTs, EUCs, INR, and these have been reviewed by the AOD clinician.
  • Mild to moderate alcohol withdrawal severity expected, defined by:
    • Average or low risk on Prediction of Alcohol Withdrawal Severity Scale (PAWSS), OR a clear clinical rationale exists for proceeding despite a higher score.
    • Patient’s average alcohol intake is ≤15 standard drinks per day.
  • Patient agrees to daily review at the service.
  • Patient has nominated a pharmacy or other suitable location (e.g. clinic or practice where medicines have been left) for DAILY pickup of medication, and the dispensing location has agreed.
  • Patient has a reasonably safe home environment or supervision.
  • Patient has reliable means for daily reviews (e.g., transport to review or clinician will go to patient).
  • Patient has a reasonable plan for service engagement after detox (e.g., outpatient services, rehabilitation).
  • There is no alcohol remaining in the house prior to detox commencing.

Exclusion Criteria (Contraindications)

  • Pregnancy or suspected pregnancy. (Requires specialist inpatient management).
  • Medical or psychiatric contraindications, including but not limited to: a seizure disorder, prior or current Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, history of suicide attempts, cognitive impairment, or recurrent delirium.
  • Major unmanaged hepatic or renal disease. If present, discussion with an addiction medicine specialist is required regarding regimen modification (e.g., using oxazepam instead of diazepam).
  • Patient is on regular opioids, beta-blockers, or alpha-adrenergic acting agents (e.g., clonidine, prazosin) due to potential interactions and/or masking of alcohol withdrawal symptoms.

Example Daily Protocol

Day 1 - initiation

On the morning withdrawal commences, assess the patient for early withdrawal, intoxication, or alcohol consumption in the past 8 hours. Intoxication, or consumption within the past 8 hours, is a contraindication to commencing treatment that day. Take baseline vital signs. NSWCG App 6

This rule is deliberately stricter than the inpatient pathway. An inpatient in objective withdrawal is treated without waiting on time since the last drink, because they are being observed; an ambulatory patient is not, so commencing benzodiazepines alongside recent alcohol is not safe unsupervised. NSWCG App 6

Thereafter, this is an example process for a structured outpatient detox program.

  • All detoxes start on Monday, cease on Friday, and are organised the week prior.
  • Scripts for the weaning regimen are written by the patient's GP or a service medical officer.
  • Patient must present daily for review:
    • Nursing staff perform a CIWA-Ar assessment in the AM before the first dose of the day.
    • Nursing staff perform a brief delirium screen daily (e.g., 4AT screen).
    • Nursing staff perform a breathalyser test (or if unavailable, an alternative screening method for recent alcohol consumption). All patients must have an undetectable BAL to qualify for further benzodiazepines. NSWCG App 6
    • Basic observations are taken (HR, BP, RR, temperature).
    • If all checks are satisfactory, staff will call the nominated pharmacy to authorise the dispensing of the next day's supply of medication.
    • In the absence of complicating factors, reviews on days 4-5 may be via phone or video call.
  • Medical Officer Review Triggers:
    • Nursing staff should call the AOD medical officer if the CIWA-Ar score is >20 (or AWS >14) or other complications arise. NSWCG Table 5.6
    • If any concerns arise (e.g., high CIWA-Ar score, positive BAL, signs of delirium), the patient should be referred to a medical officer for review and the detox protocol should be ceased.
    • The medical officer should consider calling a specialist service if withdrawal appears severe despite treatment - DASAS 1800 023 687 (regional, rural and remote NSW) or (02) 8382 1006 (Sydney metropolitan area). NSWCG §2.6
    • Top-up doses may be considered in rare circumstances via a separate, specific script.

Example Diazepam Regimen (Fixed-Dose):

A common approach is a 5-day fixed-dose weaning regimen. This must be adjusted based on clinical assessment. Staged, daily supply from a pharmacy is essential.

  • Day 3: 10mg BD (twice a day)
  • Day 4: 5mg BD (twice a day)

The retrospective week method

  1. Start from the most recent use and work backwards.
  2. Link consumption to activities - what the patient was doing, who they were with, where they were. People recall events more reliably than quantities.
  3. Cover each day of the past week, one at a time.
  4. Then ask whether that week was typical. If it was not, ask what a typical week looks like and what made this one different.

Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome

Onset: 6-24 hours (short-acting, e.g., heroin), 36-72 hours (long-acting, e.g., methadone).

Duration: 5-10 days (short-acting), 14-21 days (long-acting), with protracted symptoms lasting weeks.

Symptoms: Dysphoria, nausea, vomiting, muscle aches, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, pupillary dilation, piloerection, sweating, diarrhoea, yawning, fever, insomnia.

Key Distinguishing Features: Piloerection ("gooseflesh"), prominent yawning, pupillary dilation, rhinorrhoea, and lacrimation.

⚠️ Pregnancy Warning: Withdrawing a pregnant patient from opioids carries a high risk of fetal distress and spontaneous abortion. Do not commence withdrawal. Consult Obstetrics and Addiction Medicine immediately to arrange Opioid Substitution Therapy. Neonates born to mothers on OST will require monitoring for Neonatal Abstinence Syndrome (NAS).

1. Treatment approach: OAT, or time-limited withdrawal?

Consider Opioid Agonist Treatment (OAT) before withdrawal. Withdrawal alone rarely produces long-term change in opioid use, and carries an increased overdose risk from loss of tolerance. OAT - ongoing buprenorphine or methadone maintenance - has the stronger evidence base: it reduces mortality and retains patients in treatment, whereas standalone withdrawal has poorer long-term outcomes. Patients often ask for withdrawal without having weighed the alternative - discuss OAT, and consult addiction medicine or the local Opioid Treatment Program (OTP), before committing to a withdrawal-only plan. OTHER - NSW Health, NSW Clinical Guidelines: Treatment of Opioid Dependence (2018), §1.2.2, Table 1

Opioid withdrawal itself is intensely uncomfortable but rarely life-threatening. Consult addiction services for complex patients, pregnancy, or severe medical co-morbidities.

Choosing an agent

Buprenorphine is preferred for withdrawal from other opioids. The pragmatic approach for a patient already on methadone is to use methadone. NSWCG §8.3.5

3. Buprenorphine

🚨 Precipitated Withdrawal Warning: Administering Buprenorphine before the patient is in adequate withdrawal, or too soon after full-agonist opioids, will strip the receptors and plunge the patient into violent, sudden withdrawal.

Buprenorphine is highly effective for managing withdrawal. Micro-dosing schedules and Long-Acting Injectable Buprenorphine (LAIB) (e.g., Buvidal, Sublocade) are increasingly used and may be considered in consultation with specialist services.

Induction (Day 1)

The same induction technique applies whether the patient will continue as OAT or move onto a time-limited taper - what differs is what happens from Day 2. Decide the goal with the patient before Day 1 if possible.

  1. Defer the first dose until objective withdrawal (COWS ≥ 8). NSWCG §8.3.6
  2. Give a 2mg SL test dose. NSWCG §8.3.6
  3. Review at 1 hour. If there is no increase in severity and the patient is still in withdrawal, give a further 6mg. NSWCG §8.3.6
  4. Day 1 total: 8-12mg outpatient, 8-16mg inpatient. NSWCG §8.3.6
⚠️ Recognising precipitated withdrawal. Suspect it if there is a sustained marked increase within 1 hour of the first SL dose (COWS increase > 6, or SOWS increase > 8). Onset or worsening more than 6 hours after dosing is more likely to reflect under-dosing, not precipitation - and the two call for opposite responses. NSWCG §8.3.6

4. Methadone

Methadone's pharmacology makes induction slower and more overdose-prone than buprenorphine: it is sedating, has a long half-life (20-36 hours) that accumulates over the first 4-7 days before reaching steady state, and has a delayed peak effect (2-3 hours after dosing) - so a dose that feels adequate on Day 1 can be too much once it has accumulated. OTHER - NSW Health, NSW Clinical Guidelines: Treatment of Opioid Dependence (2018), §2.4.3

🚨 Overdose risk is highest in the first 1-2 weeks. Do not increase the dose faster than the review schedule below, even if the patient asks for more - a dose that seems insufficient on Day 2 may be more than enough by Day 5, once it has accumulated.
Regimen Recommendations and consideration
Patient supervision and review All doses should be supervised.* A clinician should review the patient daily before dosing during the first week - the greatest risk period for methadone-related overdose - assessing intoxication or withdrawal symptoms, side effects, ongoing cravings, other substance use and general wellbeing. Assess for intoxication around 2-3 hours after a dose (peak effect), and for withdrawal control at 24 hours. Do not assume what is prescribed is what is being taken.
Starting dose of methadone Commence with 20-30mg daily. Lower doses (<20mg) suit low or uncertain dependence, high-risk polydrug use (alcohol, benzodiazepines), or other severe medical complications. Higher doses (30-40mg) should be considered with caution, at the prescriber's discretion. Specialist consultation is required before starting above 40mg, given the overdose risk.

This typically results in doses of 30-50mg by the end of week 1, and 40-60mg by the end of week 2. Doses above 80mg will markedly reduce the effect of any ongoing heroin or other opioid use, but overdose risk remains.

  • Confirm current opioid agonist treatment through the Ministry of Health - (02) 9424 5921 or (02) 9391 9944 - and contact the dosing point for a dosing history before prescribing. NSWCG §8.3.5
  • For prescription opioids, check SafeScript NSW / RTPM and My Health Record. NSWCG §8.2

9. Complications of chronic use

  • Endocrine: reduced testosterone, raised prolactin, reduced FSH/LH, oligomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea, infertility.
  • Dental disease.
  • Constipation.
  • Injecting-related infections: endocarditis, cellulitis, epidural abscess, septicaemia.

Benzodiazepine Withdrawal Syndrome

Onset: 1-2 days (short-acting, e.g., alprazolam), 2-7 days (long-acting, e.g., diazepam).

Duration: 2-4 weeks, though protracted withdrawal can last months.

Symptoms: Anxiety, insomnia, restlessness, agitation, irritability, muscle tension/spasms, tremors, diaphoresis, palpitations. Severe withdrawal can include hallucinations, psychosis, and seizures.

Key Distinguishing Features: Severe muscle tension, perceptual disturbances (extreme sensitivity to light/sound), and risk of delayed seizures.

🚨 Red Flags (Escalate Care): Monitor closely for signs of severe withdrawal, including seizures, delirium, hallucinations, or severe autonomic instability. If these occur, escalate to emergency medical care immediately.
🚨 Flumazenil Contraindication: Do not use Flumazenil (a benzodiazepine reversal agent) in patients with chronic benzodiazepine dependence. It can trigger intractable, life-threatening seizures.
⚠️ Pregnancy Warning: Benzodiazepine use and withdrawal during pregnancy carry risks of neonatal withdrawal and complications. Tapering must be managed in close consultation with Obstetric and Addiction Medicine specialists.

ODDE is the organising concept for this whole page - management differs by which side of the line the patient sits on. NSWCG §11

  • Low-dose therapeutic dependence: ≤ 10mg ODDE. Reducing the original BZRA directly, without first switching to diazepam, may be appropriate. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • High-dose dependence: > 10mg ODDE. Switching to diazepam is recommended for most patients - use the equivalence table below for the conversion. NSWCG §11.3.7

🚨 Unplanned inpatient withdrawal - the commonest way this goes wrong.
  • Take a BZRA history on admission.
  • Do not abruptly discontinue, even low doses, given the risk of precipitating withdrawal in the sick and the elderly.
  • Maintain therapeutic dependence at pre-admission levels. Hospitalisation and acute illness are a poor context for elective withdrawal.

Tapering Schedules

Discussion with addiction services is strongly recommended.

Arranging a safe and effective outpatient taper is often difficult and carries risks if not closely monitored. Relapse rates are high, and abrupt cessation can lead to seizures and delirium.

Conversion to a long-acting agent (like diazepam) followed by a gradual reduction is the standard approach. For a tool to help formulate a tapering schedule, see HyperTaper.

  • There is no standard regimen. A general suggestion is a 10% reduction every 10-14 days. The actual rate depends on the starting dose, duration of therapy, relapse risk, and how well the patient tolerates tapering. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • Withdrawal may take 3 months to a year or longer, though some people withdraw in less time. Titrate to symptoms and be guided by the patient. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • In most cases a daily maximum of 40mg diazepam will prevent major withdrawal, including seizures. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • Seek specialist advice before converting to diazepam in severe hepatic dysfunction - accumulation risk; oxazepam may be preferred. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • Not supported by evidence as taper adjuncts: carbamazepine, pregabalin. NSWCG §11.3.7
  • CBT and relaxation training as adjuncts to a taper have short-term (3-month) benefit. NSWCG §11
  • Staged dispensing for community tapers is effective in both withdrawal and maintenance - e.g. daily dispensing at a local pharmacy with fortnightly clinical review. Liaison with the community pharmacist is a useful strategy. NSWCG §11.3.7

Setting

Outpatient is preferred except where safety is at risk - documented seizures, delirium, alcohol dependence, or significant untreated mental illness - where use is erratic, uncontrolled and high-dose, or involves other CNS depressants, or where outpatient success is unlikely. NSWCG §11

High-risk, high-dose patients may need to commence withdrawal as an inpatient, then continue a more gradual step-down regimen in the community after discharge. NSWCG §11.3.7

Low-dose therapeutic dependence

Withdrawal may cause more distress than continuing a stable long-term dose. Weigh that against the risks of continued prescribing - falls, cognitive decline. Stopping is not automatically the right answer. NSWCG §11

Interpreting a urine drug screen

⚠️ Temazepam and oxazepam are metabolites of diazepam. During diazepam treatment they will appear on a screen and can be misread as illicit use. Newer BZRAs such as etizolam are generally not reported unless specifically requested. A urine drug screen is an engagement tool, not a basis for punitive measures. NSWCG §11

Bridging scripts

Only with frequent review, avoiding higher-risk agents such as alprazolam, and with a clear written treatment agreement stating the arrangement is interim. NSWCG §11

Cannabis Withdrawal Syndrome

Onset: 1-2 days after cessation.

Peak: 2-6 days.

Duration: 1-3 weeks (sleep disturbances can last longer).

Symptoms: Irritability, anger, aggression, anxiety, depressed mood, restlessness, sleep difficulty (insomnia, vivid/disturbing dreams), decreased appetite/weight loss, physical symptoms (chills, sweating, shakiness, stomach pain, headache).

Key Distinguishing Features: Vivid and disturbing dreams (REM rebound), pronounced anger/irritability, and significant appetite loss.

Who withdraws

Around 50% of regular or dependent users meet the threshold for withdrawal - and 80-90% in inpatient settings, where use tends to be heavier. Daily use is the strongest predictor. NSWCG §6

Course: onset 1-2 days, peak 2-5 days, back to baseline by 7-14 days. Sleep, mood disturbance and cravings may persist for weeks. NSWCG §6

Gradual reduction or abrupt cessation?

  • Abrupt cessation suits patients who are motivated, have a supportive environment, and can tolerate a short intense period - the withdrawal is shorter overall.
  • Gradual reduction suits heavy daily users, patients who have failed abrupt cessation, and those whose circumstances (work, caring responsibilities) make a few days of marked symptoms unworkable.

NSWCG §6

Supportive care

  • Hydration 2-3 L/day.
  • Small, regular meals.
  • Avoid caffeine - it compounds the anxiety and the sleep disturbance.
  • Exercise may help mood, sleep and cravings.

NSWCG §6

Medicinal cannabis

Taper THC-based medicines over several weeks, and plan alternative management for the underlying condition before starting - otherwise the condition that prompted the prescription returns untreated mid-taper. NSWCG §6

Synthetic cannabinoids

⚠️ Not the same syndrome. Synthetic cannabinoids have higher receptor affinity, producing intense cravings and a withdrawal that may add palpitations, dyspnoea, chest pain, tachycardia and hypertension. Onset can be within an hour, peaking around day 2, over roughly a week. NSWCG §6

Psychostimulant Withdrawal (Amphetamines, Cocaine)

Onset: 1-2 days after cessation (the "crash").

Peak: 2-4 days.

Duration: 1-3 weeks.

Symptoms: Fatigue, lethargy, hypersomnia followed by insomnia, increased appetite, vivid/unpleasant dreams, psychomotor retardation or agitation, depressed mood, cravings.

Key Distinguishing Features: Profound hypersomnia, hyperphagia (increased appetite), and severe psychomotor retardation during the "crash" phase.

🚨 Red Flags (Escalate Care): The initial "crash" phase can precipitate severe psychiatric symptoms. Escalate care immediately if the patient exhibits severe paranoia, acute psychosis, or acute suicidality.

Gabapentinoid Withdrawal (Pregabalin, Gabapentin)

Onset: 1-2 days after last use. NSWCG §10

Duration: 3-5 days for severe symptoms; residual mild symptoms may persist for up to a few weeks. NSWCG §10

Symptoms: Headache, anxiety, cravings, diarrhoea, nausea, chills, fatigue. NSWCG §10

Signs: Agitation, diaphoresis, tachycardia, hypertension, tremor. NSWCG §10

⚠️ Withdrawal is not confined to high-dose or long-term use. It can occur after as little as 4 weeks of use, and at therapeutic doses (pregabalin ≤300mg, gabapentin ≤3600mg) - though most affected patients take supratherapeutic doses. Risk is increased by multiple substance dependence, higher dose, and co-occurring mental health conditions. NSWCG §10
🚨 Rare but reported: seizure, status epilepticus, catatonia, myoclonus, asterixis, akathisia, psychosis, encephalopathy. Delirium occurs with both intoxication and withdrawal. NSWCG §10

Assessment

Renal function is the key assessment, given how gabapentinoids are cleared. Seek specialist advice for significant renal impairment, and for pregnancy (Category B3). NSWCG §10

No validated withdrawal scale exists for gabapentinoids. Monitoring is clinical. NSWCG §10

Management - the taper is the mainstay

Negotiate the rate of reduction with the patient. Use a slower reduction for patients on high therapeutic or supratherapeutic doses, those on gabapentinoids for more than 4 weeks, and those with co-occurring mental health disorders or substance dependence. NSWCG §10.3.4

  • Supratherapeutic users can start the taper at maximum doses - pregabalin 600mg or gabapentin 3600mg - divided QID. Reduce or withhold if sedated. NSWCG §10.3.4
  • Where the stated dose is at or beyond maximum, begin the taper in a controlled setting and admit for at least 24 hours to monitor for toxicity from lower-than-expected tolerance - a stated dose may overstate what the patient is actually taking. NSWCG §10.3.4
  • Inpatient: taper over 5-7 days with a daily reduction. NSWCG §10.3.4
  • Unable to reach zero within 5-7 days - because of significant medical or mental health conditions, or significant distress from withdrawal symptoms - reduce to a manageable inpatient dose and continue tapering in the community rather than forcing completion. NSWCG §10.3.4
  • Hand over to community management below pregabalin 300mg/day or gabapentin 1800mg/day. NSWCG §10.3.4
  • Outpatient: reduce over 4-6 weeks with weekly reductions and staged supply. NSWCG §10.3.4

GHB/GBL Withdrawal Syndrome

Onset: 1-6 hours after last dose.

Duration: 3-14 days.

Symptoms: Tremor, anxiety, tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis, insomnia, nausea, vomiting. Severe cases can rapidly progress to delirium, severe psychosis, and seizures (similar to severe alcohol withdrawal but faster onset).

Key Distinguishing Features: Extremely rapid onset, sudden and severe delirium, wild vital sign fluctuations, and massive resistance to standard benzodiazepine doses.

🚨 Predictors of severe withdrawal or delirium. Check every one of these at assessment - they are the most useful thing to know before the patient deteriorates:
  • Dosing intervals of 2-4 hours or less.
  • More than 15 mL/day.
  • Onset of symptoms within 2-3 hours of the last dose.
  • Waking overnight to dose.
  • Previous severe withdrawal.
  • No GHB-free days for 4-6 weeks.
NSWCG §9

Onset: 1-12 hours, typically 1-4 hours. Features have been reported up to 21 days. Delirium from GHB withdrawal is a diagnosis of exclusion. NSWCG §9

No validated withdrawal scale exists for GHB. Do not use a CIWA-Ar or AWS score to drive dosing here. NSWCG §9

Management

🚨 Medical Emergency: GHB withdrawal can escalate rapidly and unpredictably. Watch for sudden onset of severe delirium, extreme agitation, and dangerous fluctuations in heart rate (bradycardia/tachycardia).

Inpatient management in a high-dependency setting is often required. Consult addiction medicine and ICU immediately. High-dose benzodiazepines are the mainstay of treatment, often requiring significantly larger doses than standard alcohol withdrawal.

Benzodiazepines - primary treatment
  • Diazepam 10-20mg every 1-2 hours, commenced early - within 2 hours of the last dose if the patient is not sedated - until light sedation and control of agitation. NSWCG §9
  • Medical officer review before exceeding 120mg diazepam in the first 24 hours; addiction specialist consultation is recommended at that point. NSWCG §9
  • ICU referral if more than 150-200mg diazepam in 24 hours, early confusion or delirium, or severe symptoms. NSWCG §9
  • Wean diazepam over approximately 7 days. NSWCG §9
  • Oxazepam is an appropriate alternative where diazepam is cautioned: severe liver disease, potent CYP450 inhibitors (e.g. ritonavir), elderly patients, head injury, severe lung disease, cerebrovascular disease, or reduced consciousness. NSWCG §9
Baclofen - adjunct

In severe cases refractory to benzodiazepines, or where moderate-severe withdrawal is predicted: baclofen 10-25mg TDS, initiated at the start of withdrawal management. Cease on discharge, or taper over several weeks. If continued, dispense weekly from a community pharmacy given the overdose risk. NSWCG §9

Antipsychotics

Olanzapine 2.5-5mg PRN, maximum 20mg/24 hours; or quetiapine 25-50mg PRN, maximum 200mg/24 hours. NSWCG §9

Monitoring

Vital signs, fluid balance, FBC, electrolytes, LFT, BAL, and creatine kinase. Hourly or more frequent in severe cases. NSWCG §9

Nicotine Withdrawal Syndrome

LOCAL - rationale: the NSW Clinical Guidance deliberately excludes nicotine and defers to the separate NSW Health tobacco and nicotine dependence guidance, so this page is drawn from that guidance and local practice rather than from NSWCG.

Onset: 2-12 hours.

Peak: 1-3 days.

Duration: 3-4 weeks.

Symptoms: Cravings, irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, restlessness, sleep disturbances, increased appetite/weight gain, depressed mood.

Key Distinguishing Features: Intense cravings, increased appetite, and weight gain.

Management

Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT) in a combination approach (e.g., long-acting patch + fast-acting oral form) is first-line. Alternatives include Varenicline or Bupropion. Offer brief interventions and referral to Quitline (13 7848).

Choosing an initial NRT product and dose

Under-dosing is a recognised problem with NRT - those wanting to quit often do not use enough to relieve cravings and withdrawal. Assess time to first cigarette and cigarettes per day to select an adequate starting dose.

More than 30 minutes after waking 2mg or 1.5mg lozenge, or 2mg gum, or 1mg spray, or 15mg inhalator 21mg/24-hour patch, plus 2mg gum, or 2mg or 1.5mg lozenge, or 1mg spray, or 15mg inhalator

Special Considerations
⚠️ Drug Interactions (CYP1A2): Tobacco smoke induces the CYP1A2 enzyme. Abrupt smoking cessation can significantly increase blood levels of medications metabolized by this enzyme, particularly olanzapine, clozapine, theophylline, and warfarin. Monitor closely for toxicity and consider prophylactic dose reductions in consultation with pharmacy/psychiatry.

Pregnancy: NRT is generally safer than smoking, though intermittent rapid-acting NRT (gum, lozenge) is often preferred over continuous patches. Consult local guidelines.
Vaping: Liquid nicotine vaporisers (e-cigarettes) may be considered as a second-line harm reduction step-down tool for patients unsuccessful with standard NRT.

Continuing Care and Relapse Prevention

Withdrawal is not treatment - it is what makes treatment possible. The Prerequisites tab requires a post-withdrawal plan; this is what one is built from. NSWCG §4.5

🚨 Tolerance is lowest at the point of completion. Every withdrawal episode ends with the patient at their most vulnerable to overdose. Safety planning and an explicit reduced-tolerance warning belong in the discharge conversation, not the discharge letter. NSWCG §4.5.1-4.5.2

Alcohol use disorder pharmacotherapy

Relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy for alcohol use disorder - naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram - should be considered for every patient completing alcohol withdrawal. For dosing, contraindications and selection, see the Australian Guidelines for the Treatment of Alcohol Problems. NSWCG §5.5

Opioids

  • Long-term opioid agonist treatment has a robust evidence base and is often preferred by patients. NSWCG §8.3.5
  • Oral naltrexone is not supported for relapse prevention. NSWCG §8.4
  • Naltrexone implants are not approved in Australia. NSWCG §8.4
  • Rapid naltrexone-assisted withdrawal is associated with high rates of adverse events and cannot be recommended. NSWCG §8.4

Diagnostic Criteria for Alcohol Withdrawal

DSM-5 Criteria

A. Cessation of (or reduction in) alcohol use that has been heavy and prolonged.

B. Two (or more) of the following, developing within several hours to a few days after the cessation of (or reduction in) alcohol use described in Criterion A:

  1. Autonomic hyperactivity (e.g., sweating or pulse rate greater than 100 bpm).
  2. Increased hand tremor.
  3. Insomnia.
  4. Nausea or vomiting.
  5. Transient visual, tactile, or auditory hallucinations or illusions.
  6. Psychomotor agitation.
  7. Anxiety.
  8. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures.

C. The signs or symptoms in Criterion B cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.

D. The signs or symptoms are not attributable to another medical condition and are not better explained by another mental disorder, including intoxication or withdrawal from another substance.

Specify if:
With perceptual disturbances: This specifier applies in the rare instance when hallucinations (usually visual or tactile) occur with intact reality testing, or auditory, visual, or tactile illusions occur in the absence of a delirium.

ICD-11 Criteria

Essential (Required) Features:

The presence of a clinically significant cluster of symptoms, behaviours, and/or physiological features that occurs upon cessation or reduction in the use of alcohol in individuals who have developed dependence on alcohol or have used alcohol for a prolonged period or in large amounts.

Presenting features of Alcohol Withdrawal may include autonomic hyperactivity (e.g., tachycardia, hypertension, perspiration), increased hand tremor, nausea, retching or vomiting, insomnia, anxiety, psychomotor agitation, depressed or dysphoric mood, transient visual, tactile or auditory illusions or hallucinations, and distractibility. Less commonly, Alcohol Withdrawal is complicated by seizures.

Onset of Alcohol Withdrawal typically occurs within 6 to 12 hours after last use, as blood alcohol concentrations decline. Symptoms vary in type, severity, onset and duration according to the duration and intensity of alcohol use prior to cessation or reduction of use.

The symptoms are not better accounted for by another medical condition or another mental disorder.
